Name Things Well: Variables and Comments That Help You Think
As someone who has led product and engineering teams in edTech, I’ve seen countless codebases grow in ways that make even seasoned developers pause. Features ship. Product scale. Users engage. However, the moment a new engineer or even the original author returns to touch the code months later, it can feel like deciphering a foreign language. And often, the culprit isn’t architecture or frameworks, it’s how we name things.
Why naming matters more than you think
In code, names carry meaning. A variable, a function, a class, these aren’t just labels. They’re how we put our thinking into the code, how we make the logic readable for ourselves and anyone who comes after us. When you choose names well, you externalize your reasoning. You save your future self and your teammates from unnecessary cognitive load.
Take a common scenario: you’re debugging a Python script. You encounter a variable called temp. Is that a temperature reading? A temporary placeholder? A timestamp? Without context, your brain wastes precious cycles guessing, tracing, and second-guessing. Rename it to averageTemperature or sessionExpiryTimestamp, and suddenly, your code starts telling a story.
It’s not just about variables. Function names, class names, constants, and configuration flags, they’re all micro-conversations with your future self. Clear naming reduces mental friction and lets you focus on what actually matters: solving problems for learners, not guessing what the code is supposed to do.
Why comments are still essential
Good naming does not replace comments; it complements them. Comments are for why something exists, not what it does. The what should be evident from your name; the why often isn’t.
For example, maxRetryCount = 5 is self-explanatory. But why 5? That’s where a comment adds clarity:
# Allow up to 5 retries because the API rate limit resets every 30 seconds
maxRetryCount = 5
Here, the combination of precise naming and contextual comments tells a story. Another engineer or even your future self doesn’t have to reverse-engineer assumptions; they can act directly on intent.
Real-world lessons
At Meta, I worked on distributed storage systems where naming consistency wasn’t just a nicety, it was critical. A single misnamed buffer could introduce data loss scenarios or subtle race conditions. Clear names and thoughtful comments prevented hours of debugging and saved terabytes of data integrity headaches.
At Educative, our learners face a similar challenge daily. When we teach Python or JavaScript, naming isn’t just a style point it’s a learning scaffold. Variables like userProgress or sessionTimeout reduce ambiguity, allowing learners to focus on the logic rather than deciphering arbitrary labels like 'x' or 'temp'.
practical tips for better naming and commenting
Be specific: Use descriptive names that convey meaning without being verbose. numActiveUsers > n.


Be consistent: Stick to naming conventions across your codebase; snake_case vs. camelCase matters.


Explain decisions: Use comments to clarify rationale, not restate code.


Iterate: Naming is rarely perfect on the first try. Refactor when context changes.


Think in the reader’s shoes: Imagine a new team member reviewing your code tomorrow. Will they get it?


Conclusion: A human-centric approach to code clarity
Naming and commenting might seem mundane, but they are powerful tools for human-centered engineering. They reduce cognitive overhead, prevent errors, and foster collaboration. When you think about names and comments as part of your product design an interface not just for machines but for humans you realize their impact on the developer experience, team velocity, and product quality.
The emotional payoff is real: clarity breeds confidence. You stop second-guessing your own work, and you empower your teammates to act decisively. Pragmatically, it saves hours of debugging, reduces onboarding friction, and makes your systems resilient to change.
Acting on this today doesn’t require sweeping architectural changes. Start by reviewing your variables and functions in a critical light. Ask: Does this name communicate intent? Would I understand it in three months? Could a new teammate read this and know the rationale? Small, deliberate adjustments accumulate into a culture of clarity.
Above all, remember this: code is for humans first, computers second. When you treat your future self and your team as primary users of your code, your systems become easier to understand, maintain, and scale. You make it easier for people to learn, solve problems, and build new things rather than just moving data around.”
Clarity isn’t just a coding habit it’s a mindset. Start naming things well, comment with intent, and let your code think alongside you.
